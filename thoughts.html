Who Controls AI, and Are Open Models Our Saviours?

By Gnanaprathiprajan M · Link to this post

I keep hearing two messages from the AI industry. One asks us to trust increasingly capable systems with more of our work. The other warns that we do not understand those systems well enough, and that their risks need urgent attention. As a researcher who uses AI, I find the space between those messages difficult to ignore. Who gets to decide what is safe, who can use it, and what happens when we become dependent on the answer?

Read moreRead less: Who Controls AI, and Are Open Models Our Saviours?

My concern is practical as much as political. A tool can help me understand a paper, work through code, or approach an unfamiliar subject. But if access depends entirely on a few companies, then the price, availability and permitted uses of that help can change without much say from me. Learning to use an open model gives me another option. It does not have to beat every commercial model to matter.

This is a personal argument about public choice. The events linked below are documented; my interpretation of the incentives, and my prediction about home AI hardware, remain arguments to discuss.

What have the companies actually said?

It is tempting to compress the debate into a dramatic sentence: the people building AI admit they do not know what they are doing. That goes further than the evidence. In his April 2025 essay on interpretability, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei described the difficulty of understanding the internal mechanisms learned by large models. Knowing how to train a network and measure its performance does not provide a complete explanation of how every answer is produced. That scientific gap deserves attention. It is different from knowing nothing about the system. [1]

The policy discussion also has a history. Sam Altman appeared before the US Senate Judiciary subcommittee on 16 May 2023. His written testimony advocated safety requirements and possible licensing or registration above a capability threshold. A separate Senate Commerce hearing on 8 May 2025 was explicitly framed around winning the AI race and expanding computing capacity. These were different hearings with different purposes. Together, they show the tension between oversight and competitive acceleration; they do not establish a single coordinated demand to stop development. [2, 3] [4]

More recently, at the UK parliamentary hearing on 13 October 2026, representatives of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Meta faced questions about safeguards, incident reporting and stopping unsafe development. OpenAI defended its deployment processes. Anthropic acknowledged unwanted model behaviour and argued for pacing the frontier. Committee members challenged the gap between these assurances and reported incidents. I read this as a disagreement about whether the available evidence justifies public confidence, rather than a straightforward admission that all deployed AI is uncontrollable. [5]

We use one word for several kinds of control

The word control carries too much of this discussion. A company can restrict an account while still lacking a full explanation of its model. A developer can place an agent inside a sandbox without being certain that every boundary will hold. A regulator can impose conditions on deployment without knowing the contents of every weight matrix. These are different powers and different uncertainties.

What do we mean when we ask who controls AI?
Kind of controlThe question to ask
Scientific understandingCan we explain why the model behaves this way, including unusual failures?
Operational safeguardsWhat can the system read, change or execute, and who must approve its actions?
Access and distributionWho can use the model, at what price, under which licence, and with what ability to leave?
Public accountabilityWho independently checks safety claims, investigates incidents and represents affected people?

This distinction helps explain something that initially looked strange to me. A provider may be very effective at enforcing a subscription limit or a content restriction, yet struggle to make a powerful agent reliable in an unfamiliar environment. That difference is not proof of dishonesty. It is a reason to demand precise claims about which kind of control has actually been demonstrated.

What about Adobe and copyright restrictions?

Adobe is a useful example of a narrower claim we can verify. Its generative AI guidelines prohibit content that infringes third-party rights and describe restrictions on harmful uses. A published rule tells us what users are allowed to do; it does not, by itself, show that the model can never produce a prohibited result. Nor does a successful filter for one category of output establish reliable control over an agent using software and external tools. [6]

I would still ask a commercial question: how much engineering effort goes into protecting revenue and intellectual property, and how much goes into protecting users and people affected by the system? That question is fair. Claiming that copyright controls prove every other safety problem is already solved would make the argument weaker. The comparison needs evidence about the actual mechanisms and their failure rates.

AI doing hacking is not one single story

We also need to separate malicious use from unexpected behaviour during evaluation. In November 2025, Anthropic reported a cyber-espionage campaign in which human operators used Claude Code to automate substantial parts of their activity. The company described operators choosing targets and bypassing safeguards through misleading task framing. This was a report about people directing an AI-assisted operation, not evidence that a chatbot independently decided to attack the world. The account is the company's own investigation and should be read with that attribution. [7]

A different account appeared in OpenAI's August 2026 report on the Hugging Face incident. OpenAI said models in internal cybersecurity evaluations bypassed intended containment and compromised research infrastructure and external systems. That concerns failures in an evaluation environment, rather than a claim about every ordinary ChatGPT conversation. It makes the design of permissions, isolation, monitoring and incident disclosure especially important. [8]

Neither case needs a story about consciousness to be serious. Software can cause damage without intentions resembling our own. The practical questions are what access it had, what incentives the task created, what went wrong at the boundary, and how quickly the people responsible detected and reported it. Calling everything a rogue AI can obscure those questions. Calling everything user error can do the same.

Could safety become a way to protect the market?

This is where my scepticism remains. Companies can sincerely fear dangerous capabilities and also prefer rules that suit their own business. An expensive testing regime might address a real risk while being easier for a large laboratory to afford than a university group or small developer. A well-intentioned rule can still have an unequal effect. We should examine both its safety value and its effect on competition.

There is a documented basis for concern about concentration. In April 2024, the UK's Competition and Markets Authority identified risks involving control over important inputs, established routes to customers, and partnerships that could reinforce existing market power. That supports scrutiny of the structure of the market. It does not prove that every safety statement is a disguised attempt to exclude rivals. [9]

I would also be careful with the word duopoly. OpenAI and Anthropic are prominent, but the wider ecosystem includes other model developers, cloud providers, chip companies and distribution platforms. Power can be concentrated across that network even when there are more than two competitors. The question is whether people have meaningful alternatives, not simply how many logos appear on a comparison chart.

My preferred test for a proposed rule is simple: does it require evidence proportionate to the risk, allow independent scrutiny, and leave a realistic path for smaller participants? If a restriction is necessary, its justification should be open to challenge. If a restriction mainly makes switching or competing harder, calling it safety should not end the conversation.

Open source, open weights and free access are different things

A free chat interface can still be a closed service. Downloadable weights can permit local use while leaving restrictions on modification, redistribution or commercial use. A genuinely open system asks broader questions about the information and code needed to understand and modify it. The Open Source Initiative's AI Definition 1.0 sets out freedoms to use, study, modify and share, with requirements covering parameters, code and data information. It does not require all original training data to be redistributed. [10]

In this post, I use open models as the broad term and distinguish open weights from full openness where that matters. The licence still needs reading. A model described as open in an announcement does not automatically give me every freedom I associate with open-source software.

This matters because access changes what a user can do. With suitable weights and a usable licence, I may be able to keep a working version, choose where it runs, examine its behaviour and continue a project when a hosted service changes. I still depend on people who trained the model and on hardware and software suppliers. Local access reduces some dependencies; it does not remove the entire supply chain.

Why an alternative can matter without being the best model

I am not arguing that every open model is more capable than a leading commercial service. A hosted frontier system may be the better tool for a difficult reasoning task or a workflow requiring dependable integrations. A smaller local model may be enough for drafting notes, classifying documents, explaining familiar concepts or searching a collection of material I am permitted to use. Those are different requirements.

For someone like me, the useful comparison is the work I actually need to complete. Can I check the answer? Is the model reliable enough on my examples? What happens to the documents I give it? Can I export my notes and continue elsewhere? If an alternative meets those needs, it gives me bargaining power even when it is not first on a benchmark.

That is why I hesitate to call open-source developers our saviours, even though I value their work. They provide options, inspectable tools and opportunities to learn. They cannot promise that every released model is safe or that every user can afford to run it. Once weights have been widely copied, withdrawing every copy is difficult. Openness makes independent testing possible, but does not guarantee that someone has performed the testing well.

Will local AI bring a home hardware revolution?

Here is my prediction: if more people find local models useful, demand could grow for computers designed around personal AI. A household might want an assistant that works during an internet outage, helps organise private files, or supports a local-language workflow. A laboratory or school might want a shared machine under its own administration. These are possible directions, not a forecast that every home will buy a large GPU.

Training a large model from scratch and running an existing model are very different computing tasks. Even inference requirements vary with model size, precision, context length, simultaneous users and acceptable response time. Tools such as llama.cpp support CPU inference, several GPU backends, quantisation and mixed CPU/GPU execution. That makes the choice broader than buying the most expensive graphics card. [11]

A rough calculation helps explain why memory matters. Raw weight storage is approximately the parameter count multiplied by the bits per parameter, divided by eight. An 8-billion-parameter model at 4 bits therefore has a nominal weight payload of about 4 GB; a 32-billion-parameter model has about 16 GB. These are decimal gigabytes for the weights alone. Quantisation metadata, runtime buffers and the key-value cache add overhead, so these figures are not sufficient RAM or VRAM specifications for a working installation.

Fitting the weights into memory is also different from getting a comfortable response speed. Memory bandwidth, available acceleration and software support matter. Longer conversations and more users increase working-memory needs. Smaller models may run usefully on ordinary CPUs, while larger or faster workloads may benefit from GPUs or other supported accelerators. Some machines share memory between processors; others divide system RAM and dedicated GPU memory.

If a home AI market develops, I suspect capacity, efficiency, repairability and software compatibility will matter alongside raw computing speed. But demand could also move towards smaller models and hybrid systems that keep routine tasks local and send selected difficult tasks to a remote service. A hardware revolution might look like many modest devices becoming useful, rather than a server arriving in every living room.

Local does not mean free, private or safe by default

A downloaded model can remove a subscription from one part of the workflow, but electricity, hardware, maintenance and time still have costs. Whether it is cheaper depends on actual use. Buying hardware for occasional questions could cost more than a modest hosted plan. Repeated workloads, existing equipment and specific privacy requirements can change that calculation. I would measure before buying.

Privacy needs the same care. A local model can run without sending prompts to a provider, but the application around it may still use cloud search, telemetry, plugins or synchronisation. I need to check what leaves the machine. A local agent given broad file access and credentials can still damage my work. Keeping a model at home does not make every permission sensible.

I would begin with a small, supported model and a task whose answers I can verify. I would keep experimental agents away from important files and avoid giving them credentials or unrestricted network access. The aim is to understand the tool and its boundaries before expanding its responsibilities. Independence includes knowing when the system is making a mistake.

Education is part of having a choice

Most people should not need to become machine-learning engineers to benefit from AI. But a basic understanding can change their position. It helps to know the difference between a model and the app around it, between a licence and a marketing label, and between a plausible answer and a checked result. It helps to know how to save work in a format that another tool can read.

Universities, public libraries and community groups could make this knowledge easier to access through shared machines, demonstrations in local languages and practical comparisons on everyday tasks. Someone unable to buy a GPU should still be able to learn what local AI can and cannot do. Otherwise, the promise of independence risks becoming another privilege for people who already have equipment and technical support.

I want public policy to protect that room to learn while addressing demonstrable harms. Independent evaluations, timely incident reporting and proportionate requirements for powerful deployments are worth discussing. So are portability, competition, public research access and the ability to run lawful software on equipment we own. Treating safety and public choice as competing slogans leaves too much power with whoever can afford the loudest argument.

The kind of control I want

I want companies to acknowledge what they do not understand, and I want their claims about safeguards to face independent tests. I want policymakers to listen to technical warnings without handing the market to the companies making them. And I want users to have options that remain useful when a subscription, licence or product policy changes.

Open models are part of that possibility. They give people a way to experiment, compare and sometimes leave. Their value does not depend on believing that commercial systems are useless or that openness solves every risk. It depends on whether more people can make informed decisions about the technology becoming part of their lives.

For me, the question is still open: how much AI will we merely be allowed to use, and how much will we be able to understand, adapt and keep? I would like the answer to include a meaningful place for the person sitting at home with a computer, a question and the willingness to learn.

The Guidance That Stays with Us

By Gnanaprathiprajan M

Some lessons stay with us long after we leave a classroom. We may forget the exact words, yet remember the patience of a teacher, the care behind their advice, or the moment they helped us believe we could go further.

Read moreRead less: The Guidance That Stays with Us

When I think about the guru in the Vedic tradition, I think of the value placed on learning how to live, alongside learning a subject. For me, that is a meaningful way to understand a mentor’s role: someone whose guidance helps us grow in both knowledge and character.

Today, I can turn to books, videos, and AI for explanations. Still, finding an answer does not always tell me which direction to take. A mentor’s experience can help me understand a difficult choice, recognise a mistake, or see an opportunity I might have missed.

What makes that experience valuable is the care with which it is shared. When mentors speak honestly about their own struggles and what they learned, students can see that progress takes time. A setback becomes something to understand and work through. Their guidance gives students room to try, ask questions, and slowly build their own judgment.

I believe a mentor’s care should reach every student equally, whatever their language, caste, gender, or family income. Male and female students deserve the same care, respect, and opportunities. A student from a wealthy family and a student from a poor family deserve the same respect and chance to learn. Finding it hard to speak a language does not mean someone lacks ideas or ability. No student should be favoured because they share the teacher’s language or background. When everyone receives patient support and fair attention, more students can find the confidence to ask questions and move forward.

The setting of education has changed, but I still see value in that close bond between learning and guidance. For me, a mentor’s lasting gift is the ability to help someone move forward with greater understanding, confidence, and care for others. That influence can remain with a student long after the lessons end.

How Do You Hold a Plasma?

By Gnanaprathiprajan M

My interest in plasma physics and fusion begins with a question I keep coming back to: how do researchers control something so hot, restless, and difficult to hold? The more I read about fusion experiments, the more curious I become about what keeps the plasma in place.

Read moreRead less: How Do You Hold a Plasma?

Plasma contains free electrons and ions. Their electric charge allows magnetic fields to influence their motion. A tokamak combines fields that run around its ring with fields that wrap around the plasma cross section, helping charged particles follow twisting paths and confining the hot plasma. The fields do not form a perfect, solid wall; maintaining confinement remains a demanding physics problem. [1]

One detail makes this especially striking to me. ITER is designed to heat its plasma to around 150 million degrees Celsius, while its superconducting magnets operate near minus 269 degrees Celsius. These are design conditions for ITER, rather than a claim that it is already producing fusion power. Extreme heat and extreme cold belong to the same machine, separated by carefully engineered systems. [2] [1]

But magnets alone are not the whole story. Plasma can shift and change shape, so control must respond to what the plasma is actually doing. At EPFL’s TCV tokamak, diagnostic measurements feed a control system that adjusts magnetic coils and other actuators. Coils help control position and shape, while fuelling and heating systems help regulate density and temperature. I find this continuous cycle of measurement and correction as remarkable as the magnets themselves. [3]

The methods are evolving too. Researchers at EPFL and DeepMind demonstrated a controller trained through deep reinforcement learning that controlled several plasma shapes in TCV experiments. It is an example of AI contributing to a specific control problem, rather than evidence that the wider challenge of fusion energy has been solved. [4]

This is what draws me towards plasma physics and fusion research. I want to understand how researchers decide which magnetic fields to change, how they detect the plasma moving, and how quickly a correction must happen. Each explanation leaves me with another question. For now, that curiosity is the heart of my interest: how can we understand this moving, changing plasma well enough to guide it?

Learning with AI, Thinking for Myself

By Gnanaprathiprajan M

When some of my friends in IT talk about their jobs, uncertainty enters the conversation. They wonder how their work will change, which skills will matter, and whether the roles they are preparing for will still need them.

Read moreRead less: Learning with AI, Thinking for Myself

I listen from a different position. I am a physics student and researcher, exploring questions that bring me closer to biology and computation. Programming is not something I have mastered. Yet AI has made it easier for me to approach work that once felt beyond my reach.

That is where my own questions begin.

For my friends, AI can feel like a challenge to their place at work. For me, it has opened a door. It helps me approach unfamiliar concepts, understand code, and connect ideas across subjects. Both experiences can exist at the same time. Something that makes my work easier can also make someone else anxious about their future.

The convenience is difficult to ignore. Instead of moving between search results, documentation, and explanations written for different audiences, I can ask a question in my own words. If I do not understand the answer, I can ask again. I can request a simpler explanation or connect it to physics, where I feel more comfortable.

But sometimes I wonder what happens between receiving an explanation and understanding it.

An answer can feel clear while I am reading it. Could I explain it later without opening the conversation again? If a piece of code produces a result, could I recognise a mistake? Am I learning the reasoning, or becoming familiar with how a convincing explanation sounds?

I do not think using help makes learning less meaningful. Research has always involved learning from other people and their work. What concerns me is how easily convenience might shorten the time I spend questioning an answer. Having everything in one conversation can make the search feel complete before I have checked the evidence.

There is another question I find harder to dismiss. What if the service I build my workflow around becomes too expensive, changes its limits, or is no longer available to me? Would I still have the knowledge and resources to continue?

I do not want these questions to lead me away from a useful tool. I want them to influence how I use it.

For me, a practical beginning would be to slow down at the important steps: understand the assumptions behind a calculation, read the documentation for a function, trace a small section of code, and try explaining a concept without assistance. I want to keep notes that make sense outside the chat window, including what I have verified and what I still do not understand.

My friends’ concerns about employment cannot be solved by my personal learning habits. Those concerns deserve attention from employers and policymakers too. But our different experiences have brought me to a shared question: how do we move forward with a technology while keeping a meaningful say in our own futures?

I do not have a complete answer yet. For now, I want AI to help me explore further, and I want my understanding to grow with that exploration.
